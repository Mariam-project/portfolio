MediTrack

A treatment-tracking app for chronic illness loses its users after a few weeks, even though they keep taking their medication. Why, and what can be done?

Why this topic

I'm the kind of user who drops an app as soon as it stops making me want to use it. So when I saw that patients kept up their treatment but stopped logging it, it caught my attention: logging was the simplest part. Digging deeper, I mostly realised that outside medical appointments, patients are often alone with their treatment, with no one there day to day to help them keep going.

01 · Scoping

Léa takes her treatment, but no longer opens the app

Léa is 34 and lives with Crohn's disease. After a severe flare-up, she downloads the app, determined to be rigorous. Three weeks later, she's still taking her treatment, but she has stopped logging it.

Where Léa comes from

Léa is a fictional character, but her story comes from a situation I know well. When someone close to me had to change their dose, I managed their treatment at first. They were supposed to write down their readings every day in a paper notebook. In practice, they filled everything in just before their appointments, hoping their device had kept the values.

Problem statement

How can we reduce tracking drop-off among patients on long-term treatment, beyond the first 4 to 6 weeks?

Hypothesis

The tracking wasn't done for the patient, but for the appointment. Between two consultations, logging gave them nothing back. Hence my hypothesis: a tracking tool gets abandoned when it gives nothing back day to day.

Objectives

  • Reduce the disengagement observed at 6 weeks
  • Increase retention at 6 months
  • Aim for light but regular use, rather than intense and occasional

02 · Benchmark

One medical reference, one from outside the sector

Medisafe is a reference in treatment tracking. Strava has nothing to do with medicine, and that's deliberate: other fields often face the same problems, and have sometimes already found answers. And keeping users motivated over time is something Strava does well.

What I observed while testing Medisafe

I tested it by recreating the real treatment of someone close to me, whose dosage had changed.

  • What works: no account needed to get started. You directly enter the medication, dose, times, treatment duration and instructions. The app also reminds you when to refill, tracks other health measures and syncs with Apple Health. Everything is in one place.
  • What holds it back: the interface is very basic and can't be personalised. It doesn't make you want to invest in it over time.
  • Dosage changes: you can change the dose for a single intake or for all future ones. However, during my test, I couldn't find any history of these changes.
  • The report: it's sent by email as a CSV file. When you open it, you see the medication name, the scheduled time and the word "Taken", but no dose and no summary. For a doctor, it doesn't help understand how the treatment is going.

Medisafe stops you from forgetting. Strava helps you keep going. The goal was to keep the latter, without the competition.

04 · Specifications

What's in scope, and what isn't

Out of scope, and why

  • Reminders: in my experience, people end up ignoring them. And research shows a deeper limitation: relying on reminders helps repeat an action, but hinders habit formation (Stawarz, Cox and Blandford, CHI 2015). Improving them wouldn't solve the drop-off.
  • Telemedicine and a dashboard for healthcare staff: a deliberate choice. My problem statement focuses on patients, and these topics deserve a separate project.

Constraints

  • Explicit consent for any data sharing (GDPR)
  • No menu overload
  • No comparison between patients: everyone has a different treatment, and even two similar treatments aren't lived the same way, because everyone has their own story and context. Comparing would make no sense, and could be discouraging.

Example user story

As a patient with a chronic illness, I want a monthly summary of my consistency, so that I feel recognised for my efforts.

Acceptance criterion: the message stays positive even if the patient's health has worsened over the period.

Why delegated management can wait

It's a need I lived through myself. But personal experience isn't enough to set a priority: other patients manage their treatment alone and don't need it. In the meantime, a loved one can log into the patient's account. That workaround raises exactly the consent and security questions at stake. So the feature deserves to be designed properly, after a legal review, rather than rushed into the first version.
